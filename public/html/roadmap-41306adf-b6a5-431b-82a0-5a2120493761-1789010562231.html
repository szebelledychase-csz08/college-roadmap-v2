1  ·  Executive Summary

Your profile contains an unusually valuable combination that most political science students never assemble: strong writing and analytical ability, a genuine interest in government, and demonstrated technical fluency through competitive programming. On the surface, "I want to work in government" and "I want to work at leading tech companies" look like competing ambitions. They are not. They converge in one of the fastest-growing and best-compensated professional fields in the country: technology policy. Every major technology company — Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, Apple, OpenAI, Anthropic, NVIDIA, Stripe — now maintains a substantial public policy organization staffed by people who can read a bill, model a dataset, and explain a transformer architecture to a Senate staffer in the same afternoon. Almost nobody can do all three. Your four years at Duke should be engineered to make you one of the people who can.

The strategic architecture of this roadmap is simple: Political Science gives you the institutional literacy and writing credibility; the Mathematics minor gives you quantitative authority; your programming background gives you technical legitimacy; and Duke's Sanford School of Public Policy plus its Washington, D.C. pipeline gives you the network. You will use the Mathematics minor deliberately — not as a decoration, but to reach probability, statistics, and optimization, which are the mathematical grammar of algorithmic accountability, privacy law, antitrust economics, and AI risk regulation. You will use your competitive programming past not to become a software engineer, but to earn a seat in rooms where policy is written about systems you can actually read.

The four-year arc: Freshman year — establish a 3.7+ GPA, complete Writing 101 and calculus, join Duke Political Union and a data-oriented club, and get a first campus research assistantship. Sophomore year — declare the major and minor, complete linear algebra and probability, take your first policy analysis course, and land a summer internship in a congressional office, a state legislature, or a tech policy think tank. Junior year — the pivotal year: complete Duke in DC or a semester at Sanford's Washington program, apply for the Truman Scholarship, publish or present original research, and secure a summer internship in a major technology company's public policy team or at a federal agency (FTC, FCC, NIST, NTIA, OSTP). Senior year — convert the internship into a return offer, complete a distinction thesis on an algorithmic-governance topic, apply for the fellowship layer (TechCongress, Aspen Tech Policy Hub, Presidential Management Fellows, Rhodes/Marshall), and graduate with a job or a funded fellowship in hand. Target outcome: a $75K–$135K first role at the intersection of Washington and Silicon Valley, with a realistic path to a senior policy advisor or director role by age 32.

Five Non-Negotiable Milestones

  1. Year 1: GPA ≥ 3.7 · Writing 101 · Calculus sequence started · one faculty relationship established
  2. Year 2: Major + minor declared · first substantive policy internship · Bass Connections or faculty RA position
  3. Year 3: Semester in Washington, D.C. · Truman Scholarship application · tech-company or federal-agency internship
  4. Year 4: Distinction thesis on tech governance · full-time offer or fellowship secured by March
  5. Throughout: A public writing portfolio — 8–12 published policy pieces by graduation

2  ·  Freshman Year — Foundation & Calibration

Theme: Prove you can carry quantitative and humanistic rigor simultaneously. Build the GPA floor that makes everything else possible.

Fall Semester

Course Title Strategic Rationale
WRITING 101 Academic Writing (choose a technology-, law-, or democracy-themed section) Required of all first-years. Your career is a writing career — treat this as professional training, not a checkbox.
POLSCI 145D Introduction to Political Economy (or POLSCI 130-level American Politics gateway) Duke's political economy sequence is the most quantitative entry point into the major and aligns directly with regulatory policy.
MATH 122L / 111L Introductory Calculus with Applications (placement-dependent; skip to MATH 212 if you have BC credit) Opens the Mathematics minor. Do not delay — the minor requires a chain of prerequisites.
PUBPOL 155 Introduction to Policy Analysis (Sanford School) Your gateway into Sanford's advising network, D.C. programs, and internship pipeline — even as a Trinity Political Science major.
Foreign Language I Mandarin, Arabic, Russian, or Spanish (placement-based) Satisfies Trinity requirements and positions you for Boren/Critical Language Scholarships. Mandarin or Russian is highest-value for tech and national-security policy.

Spring Semester

Course Title Strategic Rationale
POLSCI 116D Political Institutions / American Political System Core subfield requirement. You cannot advise on policy without understanding Congress, agencies, and courts structurally.
MATH 212 / 202 Multivariable Calculus Required backbone of the Math minor; prerequisite chain for probability and optimization.
COMPSCI 201 Data Structures & Algorithms (place directly in — your competitive programming background makes CS 101 a waste of a semester) The single credential that separates you from every other poli-sci student applying to tech policy roles. Get it on the transcript early.
ECON 101 Economic Principles Prerequisite for antitrust, platform regulation, and any serious regulatory-economics work. Also a Sanford prerequisite.
Foreign Language II Continuation of Fall language Maintain momentum; language skills decay fast with gaps.

Extracurriculars & Activities

  • Duke Political Union / Duke Debate — the fastest way to build the room-presence and argumentation skill a policy advisor lives on. Commit to one, not both.
  • The Chronicle (Duke's student newspaper) — join as an opinion columnist or news writer. Goal: 4–6 published bylines by May. This begins your public writing portfolio, which is the single most underrated asset in policy hiring.
  • Duke Political Review or Duke Journal of Politics & International Affairs — apply as a staff writer; aim for an editorial position by sophomore year.
  • Duke ACM / Duke Competitive Programming Club — stay connected to your technical roots. Compete in at least one ICPC regional or Codeforces season. This is a résumé differentiator, not a hobby.
  • POLIS: Center for Political Leadership, Innovation, and Service — attend every event you can. Sign up for their listserv in Week 1; this is where D.C. opportunities are announced first.
  • Office hours discipline — visit each professor at least twice per semester. Faculty relationships built freshman year become research positions sophomore year and recommendation letters junior year.
